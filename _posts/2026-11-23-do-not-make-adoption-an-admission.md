A few years ago, I watched a team roll out a tool that made a tedious weekly report much easier to produce.

The arithmetic looked obvious. The old report took an experienced operator most of an afternoon. The new workflow assembled the inputs in minutes, left the judgment calls visible, and produced a cleaner draft. People said they wanted it. In a demo, they completed the task without much trouble.

Then most of them returned to their spreadsheets.

Our first explanations were familiar. The entry point was too quiet. The import needed work. People were busy. Each was partly true, but none explained a pattern in the conversations. The operators kept telling us how much context the old report required. They described the calls they made, the anomalies they caught, and the years it had taken to know what looked wrong.

We were selling saved time. They were explaining the value of their judgment.

Using our product did not merely mean trying a faster workflow. It risked conceding that an afternoon of visible effort had never been necessary. Worse, the launch language made the tool sound like a substitute for the craft they were proud of. Adoption had become an admission.

Growth teams are comfortable looking for monetary cost, effort, uncertainty, and missing value. I think we are less practiced at seeing **identity cost**. A person may understand the product, have permission to use it, and expect a good result while still avoiding the behavior because of what that behavior seems to say about them.

That is not irrational resistance. It is part of the product experience.

## Utility is not the whole decision

Product work often imagines adoption as a private comparison between the old way and the new one. Is the new path faster, cheaper, or better enough to justify switching?

Work is rarely that private. A behavior is also observed by a manager, a peer, a client, or the person doing it. Those audiences give the behavior meaning.

Economists George Akerlof and Rachel Kranton brought identity into an economic model by arguing that a person's sense of self can affect choices and economic outcomes. Their point is useful well beyond the model. Preferences do not arrive at the product stripped of social roles. [Their paper makes identity part of the account of behavior](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/115/3/715/1828151).

The experienced operator is not only choosing how to make a report. She may be choosing whether to behave like the kind of operator her organization respects. A sales manager using an automatic call summary may wonder whether it makes him look inattentive. A designer starting from a template may worry that speed will be read as a lack of originality. A founder accepting a suggested forecast may feel she is surrendering the judgment investors expect her to possess.

The product can create real utility and still attach the wrong social meaning to receiving it.

Self-determination theory offers another useful lens. Richard Ryan and Edward Deci describe competence, autonomy, and relatedness as needs associated with motivation and well-being. [Their review explains how social conditions can support or thwart those needs](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf).

A growth flow can press on all three without containing a single obvious usability problem.

- It can make a skilled person feel that their competence has been reduced to a button
- It can present a manager's mandate as the user's choice and quietly remove autonomy
- It can separate someone from a peer group whose shared ritual helped define good work

This is why another tooltip or a richer feature tour often does so little. The user does not lack instructions. The user lacks a way to adopt the product without losing a valued account of who they are.

## Listen for the defense of the old work

Identity cost is easy to invent after the fact. A team sees weak adoption, declares that users feel threatened, and gains an explanation that is both flattering to the product and difficult to disprove.

I would not start there.

Start with behavior you can see and language users actually use. In particular, listen for a defense of work that the new product claims to remove.

People may emphasize how much care the old process takes. They may name edge cases before discussing the common case. They may insist that the task is not merely administrative. They may use the new path privately but recreate the old ritual when a senior colleague is present. They may praise the outcome while refusing to share how it was produced.

None of those observations proves identity cost. The old path may genuinely handle important exceptions. The product may be unreliable. Incentives may reward hours rather than outcomes. A manager may have made a sensible local decision that the growth team does not understand.

But the observations are a reason to ask a better question.

Instead of asking only what makes the new workflow hard, ask what the old workflow allows the person to demonstrate.

The answer may be diligence, taste, control, care, toughness, independence, or membership in a professional group. That demonstration is part of the incumbent product, even if no one has put it on a requirements document.

## Keep a role story review

Before changing prompts, incentives, or onboarding, I would write a short role story review. It fits on one page and forces the team to separate evidence from a convenient psychological story.

### The behavior

- The specific new behavior we want to see
- The old behavior it would replace or change
- Where adoption stalls and what people do instead
- Whether the new behavior happens differently in private and in front of others

### The valued role

- The role the person is trying to perform well
- The qualities the old behavior makes visible
- The audience whose judgment matters in that moment
- The user's own words that support this interpretation

### The possible identity cost

- What adopting the product could appear to concede
- Which competence, choice, or relationship might feel diminished
- Whether the cost comes from the product, the launch language, or the surrounding organization
- What evidence would make us reject this explanation

### The dignity preserving path

- The part of the person's judgment that should remain visible
- A way to show authorship, review, or accountable choice
- Language that describes the enlarged role without insulting the old one
- A first use that can happen with the relevant audience and not only in a private demo

The phrase dignity preserving matters. This is not a search for nicer marketing copy. It is a design test.

In the reporting product, we stopped presenting the system as the thing that made the report. The first screen showed which inputs it had assembled, which anomalies required review, and where the operator had changed the draft. The final report named the reviewer and preserved a short note about the judgment behind unusual adjustments.

Those changes did not pretend the software had done less. They made the human contribution legible in the place where it still mattered.

There is a line here. A product should not manufacture ceremonial clicks just to reassure everyone that a person touched the work. Nor should it preserve a harmful hierarchy because the hierarchy gives someone status. Sometimes the honest product decision is to change the role and say so plainly.

The review is meant to reveal the trade, not automatically protect the incumbent arrangement.

## Make the identity explanation loseable

For the reporting workflow, I would write the hypothesis this way.

> We believe experienced operators who see the product preserve visible reviewer judgment will publish a first report at least fifteen percentage points more often within two weeks than comparable operators who see time-saving language, because the revised experience lets them adopt without implying that their expertise was unnecessary.

This can be wrong in useful ways. Adoption may not move. Newer operators may respond more than experienced ones. People may start a report but refuse to publish it. Interviews may show that trust in the inputs, not role meaning, remains the binding concern.

The method should make those alternatives visible.

Randomly assign eligible teams to two honest versions of the introduction. One emphasizes faster report production. The other emphasizes a prepared draft, visible exceptions, and accountable reviewer judgment. Keep capabilities, access, training time, and manager communication the same. Do not add fake scarcity or imply that either group is deficient.

Measure first report publication as the primary behavior. Also record draft starts, manual changes, time to publication, and whether the report is shared with the usual audience. Segment the result by relevant experience bands chosen before the test. The identity account predicts more than a general copy lift. It predicts that preserving the role story should matter more for people whose accumulated expertise is most implicated, and that the effect should remain at the public act of sharing rather than ending at a private draft.

Then conduct interviews after the behavioral window. Ask participants to walk through how they explained the new workflow to a colleague. Do not ask whether the product threatened their identity. That question supplies the theory and invites agreement. Listen for whether they describe the tool as replacing effort, exposing weakness, extending judgment, or making their contribution easier to show.

If both introductions perform alike and the same reliability concerns dominate, stop telling the identity story. Fix reliability. If the role-preserving version improves private starts but not publication, look at the audience around the behavior. If it works only after managers change how they evaluate the report, the intervention is organizational as well as product-led.

That last outcome is inconvenient, but it is still a useful decision. It tells us not to spend another quarter polishing an individual flow while the social reward points the other way.

## Safety changes what people are willing to try

There is a reason identity cost can hide during research. A friendly session with a product team is often safer than use at work.

Amy Edmondson defined team psychological safety as a shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk taking. In a field study of work teams, psychological safety was associated with learning behavior. [The study connects the interpersonal climate around a team with whether learning behaviors occur](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/2666999).

Trying an unfamiliar product in front of colleagues can be an interpersonal risk. The user may reveal that they do not know the old process, that they want help, or that a piece of their job can be done differently. A usability test can show that the controls make sense while missing the risk of being seen using them.

That suggests a simple addition to research. Recreate the audience, not just the task.

Ask how the participant would introduce the output in the next meeting. Let them decide what provenance should travel with it. Observe a paired trial with a real collaborator when appropriate. Compare solitary activation with the first visible use in normal work.

The goal is not to turn every product study into organizational anthropology. It is to notice when the decisive interface is between a person and the people watching, rather than between a person and the screen.

## Growth can honor the person doing the changing

Mid-career product work has made me wary of calling people resistant.

The label can be accurate, but it often ends the investigation at exactly the point where it becomes interesting. Resistance may contain information about reliability, incentives, professional standards, or a role the product is quietly rewriting.

We should not romanticize every old process. The afternoon report was not valuable because it consumed an afternoon. The operators did not need the spreadsheet preserved as a monument to effort. They needed the new system to recognize that assembling information and exercising judgment were different contributions.

That distinction changed both the product and the growth story.

The more useful promise was not that expertise no longer mattered. It was that expertise could spend less time gathering the obvious and more time resolving what was not.

When a product changes how work gets done, it also changes what good work looks like and how people show it. Ignoring that layer does not make the decision purely functional. It only leaves the social part of the product undesigned.

A strong growth path should make the better behavior easier. A thoughtful one should also let people take that path without asking them to become smaller on the way.
