A surprising amount of growth work begins after our product is already on the screen.

We improve the landing page, shorten signup, sharpen the upgrade message, and make the first action easier. These are useful changes. They answer a real question about whether someone will choose us when we are present.

They do not answer whether we will be present in the person's mind when the problem appears.

That distinction has become more important to me in the middle of my career. Teams can become excellent at converting traffic they have paid for, prompted, or pulled into a session. The dashboard looks healthier. Yet the product remains absent from the ordinary moments that should bring someone back under their own power.

I have treated that absence as a messaging problem before. Sometimes it was. More often, our message described the product category while the user remembered the situation.

We said project planning. They thought about the Monday handoff that always went sideways.

We said personal finance. They thought about opening a restaurant bill with friends.

We said document automation. They thought about the customer form that arrived with three missing fields.

Growth has to connect the product to those occasions, not only make the product persuasive once it has been found.

## Choice starts before the comparison

Marketing research often uses the idea of a consideration set, the smaller group of alternatives a person might actually choose from. The exact model is contested, which is useful caution for product teams. Horowitz and Louviere found that forcing choice into a neat consider-then-choose model could produce bad forecasts in the settings they studied. They still found that reported consideration carried information about preferences. [Their study is a reminder that consideration is useful without being a universal law](https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-8116%2895%2900004-L).

A later paper by Carson and Louviere makes the measurement problem sharper. If researchers infer the alternatives people considered only from what they ultimately chose or remembered to report, preference estimates can become biased. The mechanism that puts an option into consideration matters too. [Their paper argues that selection into the considered set cannot be treated as invisible](https://economics.ucsd.edu/~rcarson/papers/JOCMConsiderationSets14.pdf).

That is uncomfortably close to a standard growth funnel.

We observe people who searched our category, clicked our ad, opened our email, or visited our pricing page. Then we optimize which of those people choose us. We know a great deal about selection after the product entered view and much less about how it entered view at all.

This creates a quiet blind spot. A checkout improvement can raise conversion among visitors while doing nothing for the larger group who encounter the need and never think of the product. Both statements can be true. The test can be valid and the growth claim can still be too broad.

## People remember situations, not positioning decks

Memory research offers a useful way to think about this gap.

Endel Tulving and Donald Thomson's work on encoding specificity showed that retrieval depends on the relationship between how something was learned and the cue available later. In their experiments, a word that people failed to recognize in one setting could still be recalled when the earlier cue returned. [The original paper made retrieval conditions part of the explanation for remembering](https://alicekim.ca/9.ESP73.pdf).

A product analogy should not be pushed too far. A brand is not a word-list experiment. But the operating lesson travels well. The cue available when a need arises may be quite different from the language available when someone first meets the product.

A budgeting app may introduce itself with a promise about financial control. The later cue is not financial control. It is the moment a user wonders whether a weekend trip will make rent uncomfortable.

A team tool may introduce itself as an intelligent workspace. The later cue is a colleague asking which version was approved.

A meal product may sell convenience. The later cue is looking into the fridge after a late meeting.

Prospective-memory research describes remembering to perform an intended action when an environmental cue appears. A systematic review of emotional cues found that effects depended on factors including whether the cue appeared during both learning and retrieval. [The review treats the match between cue and occasion as an empirical question rather than a slogan](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29318922/).

For product teams, the point is not to manufacture emotion or turn every situation into a notification. It is to learn which naturally occurring cue should make the product relevant, then see whether the experience actually establishes that association.

## Keep an occasion recall sheet

I would add a one-page occasion recall sheet to a growth bet before changing the campaign or onboarding.

### The real occasion

- What is happening in the person's world when the need becomes noticeable
- The words they use for that situation before our category language appears
- The physical, social, or digital cue already present
- What they do today if our product does not come to mind

### The product encounter

- Where the person first sees or tries the product
- The promise and example shown there
- Whether that example resembles the real occasion
- Which memorable noun, action, or image links the product to the occasion

### The recall test

- The cue we will present without showing our name, logo, interface, or category
- What counts as unaided mention of the product
- What counts as remembering the product only after a list is shown
- Which alternative comes to mind first when ours does not

### The decision note

- The audience and occasion covered by the evidence
- The minimum change in unaided recall worth acting on
- The conversion and product-value guardrails that must also hold
- The claim we will not make from prompted awareness alone

The distinction between unaided and aided recall matters. Showing five product names and asking which one feels familiar can tell us something about recognition. It cannot tell us whether our product would have entered the choice on its own.

The sheet also prevents a common creative shortcut. Repeating the logo more loudly may improve brand recognition without strengthening the connection to a useful occasion. Familiarity is not the same as relevance at the moment of need.

## Write the retrieval claim before the creative

Imagine a shared-expense product that performs well when people search for bill-splitting apps. The team wants to increase organic return, so it plans a new onboarding sequence around simplicity and speed.

I would write a narrower hypothesis first.

> We believe showing a concrete restaurant-bill example during first use will increase the share of new users who name our product without prompting when later given that situation by at least eight percentage points, while completed splits and reported comfort with money privacy do not decline.

This can be wrong in several useful ways.

The restaurant occasion may be too narrow. The example may be memorable but associated with the wrong part of the experience. People may recall the product but still prefer a calculator, a payment app, or one friend doing the arithmetic. The example may even make the product feel invasive.

A falsifiable retrieval claim leaves room for all of those results. A statement that the new story will improve brand salience usually does not.

## Run an occasion-to-choice experiment

Recruit people who have the relevant need and have not used the product before. Randomly assign them to one of two first-use experiences.

The current experience explains the broad benefit and lets people complete a realistic split. The candidate experience supports the same task with the same functionality, but frames it around the restaurant-bill occasion using language collected from research. Keep incentives, acquisition source, task difficulty, and product access the same.

After the initial use, do not train one group more heavily or surround them with extra reminders. At a prespecified follow-up, give both groups a short set of situations in random order. Include the restaurant bill, a household subscription, a group trip, and unrelated problems. Ask what they would do in each situation before displaying any product names.

The primary measure is unaided product mention for the target occasion. Secondary measures include the first alternative named, aided recognition after the unaided question, and whether recall spills into occasions the product cannot serve well.

Then offer a realistic choice. Participants can use the product, use another method, or do nothing. Measure successful task completion and any privacy or social discomfort. Recall without choice may mean the association is memorable but not useful. Choice without a good outcome may mean growth is outrunning the product.

Set the analysis plan before responses arrive. Specify which phrases count as an unaided mention, have reviewers code blinded responses, and report disagreement rather than quietly resolving every ambiguous answer in the candidate's favor. Do not keep adding occasions until one produces a positive result.

There are also guardrails worth stating plainly.

Do not create a fear cue the product cannot responsibly resolve. Do not imply that a normal mistake is a crisis. Do not claim the product is appropriate for a sensitive financial or health situation merely because that situation is memorable. Do not retarget the candidate group between first use and the recall test, since extra exposure would change the thing being measured.

The method will not reproduce everyday life perfectly. A research prompt makes an occasion more explicit than life usually does. Recruitment itself can increase category awareness. Self-report is not behavior. That is why the realistic choice and successful outcome belong in the design. The result should be read as evidence about a specific association, not proof that the product has secured a permanent place in memory.

## The missing association can change the roadmap

An occasion recall miss is easy to hand to marketing. That may be the wrong owner.

If users remember the situation but not the product, the first encounter may be speaking at the wrong level. If they remember the product but do not choose it, the value proposition or trust boundary may be weak. If they choose it and cannot finish the job, the product is failing after retrieval. If they recall it for an occasion it should not serve, the message has created a dangerous association.

Those are different problems with different owners.

This is why I like the occasion recall sheet as a growth artifact. It sits upstream of a channel plan but downstream of a real user problem. It gives research, product, design, and marketing one concrete chain to inspect.

The occasion happened.

A cue became noticeable.

The product came to mind or it did not.

The person chose it or chose something else.

The choice produced value or it did not.

A team can improve any link in that chain. It should not claim to have improved all of them because a landing page converted better.

## Earn a place in the moment

I used to think of recall as mostly a brand concern, something to address once the product and funnel were mature.

I now think that view arrives too late.

A product becomes part of everyday life when people connect it to a real situation where it can help. That connection is partly language, partly memory, partly product truth, and partly restraint. It cannot be bought entirely with more impressions, and it cannot be inferred from conversion among people who already arrived.

The practical question is simple.

When the need appears and our interface is nowhere in sight, what will make the product come to mind?

If we cannot answer that, the funnel may be improving inside a choice we have not yet earned.
